## The Locked Garden

At the end of Maple Street, behind enormous iron gates covered in rust and ivy, was the most talked-about garden in the entire town.

Nobody had entered it in one hundred years. Nobody knew why it was locked. The giant who owned it had not been seen in all that time.

The garden itself was visible from the top of the hill — and even from a distance, it was clearly dying. Brown grass, bare trees, not a single flower.

**Lily, Marcus and Dev** had never been especially interested in the garden.

Until Marcus kicked his football over the gate.

## Inside the Gates

The gates were locked — but the children discovered a gap in the hedge just big enough to squeeze through.

They stepped inside and gasped.

The garden was enormous — easily the size of their whole school and playing field put together. And it was not entirely dead. In the very centre stood one magnificent sunflower, taller than the gate, still blazing gold in the grey surroundings.

"How is that alive when everything else is dead?" Lily wondered.

A voice like grinding stone answered her: "Because I water it every day. It is the only thing I have left."

## The Giant

He was enormous — truly enormous — seated on a stone bench near the sunflower, almost invisible under a grey cloak. His face was deeply lined, his eyes sad and distant.

The children should have run. Instead, Lily walked forward.

"Why is everything else dead?" she asked gently.

The giant was quiet for a long time. Then he said: "Because I stopped caring for it, when I stopped believing anyone would ever want to see it."

## Working Together

Marcus found a watering can. Dev spotted a pile of old seeds in a rusted tin near the wall. Lily found a rack of gardening tools, stiff with disuse.

"We could help," she said simply. "If you showed us what to do."

The giant looked at the three small children for a long time.

Then, very slowly, he reached down and picked up a trowel.

They worked all afternoon. The giant knew everything about growing things — which plants liked sun and which liked shade, how deep to plant each seed, how much water each flower needed.

The children worked hard and laughed a lot and asked hundreds of questions, and the giant answered every single one.

## Springtime

By the time the children had to go home, three new rows of seeds were planted and the whole garden had been watered.

By the following week, green shoots were pushing through the soil.

By the month after that, the garden blazed with colour — roses and tulips, lavender and honeysuckle, sunflowers and poppies stretching towards the sky.

The giant opened the gates.

The whole town came to see.

And if you walked past Maple Street on a sunny afternoon, you would see an enormous figure moving slowly between the flowers with a watering can — and usually at least three children sitting on the stone bench beside him, listening to stories about what the garden looked like a hundred years ago, when it was first planted.
